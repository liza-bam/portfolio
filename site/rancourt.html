Rebuilding a legacy shoe catalog into a Shopify store the brand’s team could run.

The shoemaker had thousands of listings, broken links, and a store that was difficult for customers and staff to use.

Problem

One of the last handsewn moccasin makers in America had a store that did not match its shoes: a catalog with no structure, stale products, empty and failing pages, poor accessibility, too many apps, a confusing menu and a confusing cart.

As UX and service designer, I ran discovery workshops with the factory team, designed the catalog model, the Shopify theme and the cart, moderated six usability sessions, built the store and taught the team to run the new catalog.

1. Discovery with the factory team

  • UX and service designer

Problem

The catalog was a flat database built on tags, with no categories and no shared fields.

Challenge

What defines a product lived in the factory’s season documents and catalogue sheets, not in the store.

What I did

Ran discovery workshops with the factory team and traced product development from design to the store, to get the full picture of what defines a “product” at Rancourt. This became the base for the catalog model.

See more: development documentation, product definition+

Before any change to the store, the search rankings it already had needed protecting.

2. Protecting search traffic

  • UX and service designer

Problem

The brand ranked first for “penny loafers” and “resole shoes”. The rebuild had to keep every one of those URLs and the words shoppers use to find them.

Challenge

Of 11.6K non-branded keywords, only 257 held a top-3 spot; most sat on page two or in SERP features, so the rankings were easy to lose.

What I did

Analysed the search data: where the rankings sat, which product type carried the equity and how shoppers say “quality”. Six pages brought in most of the organic traffic, so they kept their URLs and received the strongest content.

See more: loafers equity, how shoppers say quality, keywords worth protecting+

With the findings and the rankings in hand, the work had to be planned and scoped.

3. Plan and scope

  • UX and service designer

Problem

Every request was pushed into the first release.

Challenge

The client’s team had its own work to do, so their effort had to be planned per phase.

What I did

Planned seven phases plus hardening, a written definition of done and the client’s effort per phase: think first, prove on a sample, then migrate. Sorted every wish into one list of three tiers, launch, next and later, and colored the site map by tier so nobody had to ask why.

The first phase was the catalog itself.

4. Catalog model and navigation

  • UX and service designer

Problem

Every visible problem was a data problem underneath. Each product carried its own typed-in leather, sole, lining and care text, so the same thing had several names.

Challenge

The brand’s team had to manage the catalog on its own after launch.

What I did

Designed one product model: a product is a pattern, a colorway and an upper; variants are size and width; everything else comes from one shared library of 29 uppers, 23 soles, five linings, eight lasts and 36 colors. Select the pattern and most of the product is already filled in; rename once, every page follows. Built the navigation from the same fields: type, gender, collection and occasion. Nine top-level items became five, and Shoe Care and Repairs merged into one service area, with eligibility set per model.

The new store, built on the model, then went to shoppers.

5. User testing

  • UX and service designer

Problem

The menu used the factory’s words, which shoppers did not understand.

Challenge

Every misread label had to be logged and ranked by how many testers hit it, so the most common problems were fixed first.

What I did

Tested the new store, built to the client’s vision, with a team of user testers. Wrote the test guide and moderated six sessions: four tasks, a debrief, every misread label logged. Fourteen themes came up more than once; the ones four or more people hit were fixed before launch.

Result

Designed and built by one person, run by the brand’s team. Deliverables: a custom Shopify storefront and cart, a reusable catalog model for patterns, leathers, sizes and widths, the redirect migration and staff training.

Lessons learned

Design the data before the screens

Most problems in the old store were data problems: colours without names, products filed in the wrong category, and one leather sold under three different names. Fixing the product data first solved them at the source. A visual redesign alone would only have hidden them.

Protect existing search traffic

Search data shaped the information architecture as much as the wireframes did. Six pages brought in most of the organic traffic, so they kept their URLs and received the strongest content.

Test labels with customers, not only layouts

Six moderated sessions showed that customers did not understand several of the brand’s own terms, including “Outlet”, “Mocs”, “Leather goods” and “D width”. Navigation has to use the customer’s words, not the company’s.

Make scope visible

A three-tier site map showed what would launch first and what would follow. Once stakeholders could see what was planned next, they stopped pushing every request into the first release.
